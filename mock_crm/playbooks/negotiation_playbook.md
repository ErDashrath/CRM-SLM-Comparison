# Negotiation Playbook

**Owner:** Sales Enablement
**Applies to:** Commercial Negotiation and later pipeline stages

## Core principle: don't lead with discounting

When a deal reaches commercial negotiation and the customer opens with a price objection, the instinct is to respond with a number. Resist it. A price objection raised by procurement, without the economic buyer present or vocal, is frequently a proxy for an unresolved value question further up the org chart — not a genuine price ceiling. Leading with a discount answers the wrong question and trains the customer to negotiate on price in every future cycle, including renewals.

## Engage the economic buyer before moving on price

The economic buyer's presence (or absence) at commercial-stage meetings is one of the strongest leading indicators of deal health. If the economic buyer has skipped recent negotiation meetings, treat that as the primary risk to resolve — not a scheduling inconvenience. A discount agreed with procurement alone, without the economic buyer re-engaged, frequently fails to convert into an actual signed deal, because the person with sign-off authority was never brought back into the value conversation. Before offering any concession, get the economic buyer back in the room, even if that means slowing the timeline.

## Position on TCO and ROI, not unit price

When a competitor is used as pricing leverage, avoid a feature-by-feature or line-item price match. Reframe the conversation around total cost of ownership: implementation quality, support responsiveness, integration completeness, and the cost of a failed or delayed rollout. A lower sticker price from a competitor is rarely lower total cost once implementation risk and support quality are accounted for — make that case explicitly, ideally with the economic buyer in the room, rather than matching the number.

## Stage-appropriate next best actions

- **Technical Evaluation approved, moving to Commercial Negotiation:** Confirm the economic buyer is scheduled for the next commercial touchpoint before conceding anything on price.
- **Procurement opens with a discount ask above the approved band:** Do not confirm a number. Re-engage the economic buyer, gather any specific competitive detail (competitor name, if known, and what they're offering), and escalate per the Discount Policy approval thresholds.
- **Competitor named as pricing leverage:** Pull the relevant battlecard, position on TCO/ROI and implementation risk, and avoid matching price line-for-line.
- **No commercial response for several days after a discount request:** Don't let it go silent. A prompt, value-reinforcing follow-up (not a discounted counter-offer) keeps the deal from stalling further and re-signals that price isn't the only lever being discussed.
- **Deal has gone quiet with no next meeting scheduled and no pricing conversation underway:** This is an engagement problem, not a pricing problem — re-engagement outreach to the champion and economic buyer, not a discount, is the correct next best action.

## What "good" looks like at close

A deal that closes within the approved 5-7% band, with the economic buyer actively engaged in the final negotiation, is a materially healthier outcome than a deal that closes at a deeper discount without economic-buyer sign-off — the latter is more likely to churn or under-deliver expected value at renewal.
